\title{One Parallel Inference Phase:\\
Decentralised Inference with Swarms of\\
Small Heterogeneous Language Models on Consumer Hardware}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Decentralised LLM inference usually splits one large model across volunteer machines, which puts the
wide-area network inside every decoding step and caps throughput at a few tokens per second. We study the
opposite design: every consumer device runs a \emph{whole} small open model of a \emph{different} family,
and each request uses one parallel inference phase after peer reservation and key discovery, with local
answer fusion. Under explicit idealised assumptions we prove an exact and optimal stop certificate and
Bayes-optimal $K$-class vote weights; operational certificates use a conservative sufficient condition;
we also give an identity for the gain of a minority appeal; and incentive conditions for a credit system, including an
impossibility result for naive duplicate-and-compare audits and the indistinguishability condition under
which encrypted canary jobs restore detection. Empirically, on GSM8K a swarm of four
\eTwoSwarmSize{}B models from four families reaches \eTwoVote\% with one peer round trip, against \eTwoBest\% for its
best member observed on test and \eTwoRef\% for a \eTwoRefSize{}B model, while collaborative token-level writing costs
\eTwoCroiseTrips{} round trips without a demonstrated quality gain in this protocol. On GSM8K, MATH-500, ARC-Challenge
and MMLU-Pro, a weighted vote of
seven \eFourSwarmSize{}B models from seven families averages \eFourMeanSwarm\%, against \eFourMeanQwenNine\% for
a 9B model, \eFourMeanQwenTwentySeven\% for a 27B model and \eFourMeanGemmaTwelve\% for a 12B model. Over the
pooled test questions, exploratory unadjusted tests indicate non-inferiority to the 9B and 27B models
within two points and inferiority to the 12B one, with approximate pooling across fixed strata; the seven members hold
as many parameters as the 27B model, at most \eFourLargestPeerParams{}B per
device. In an exploratory replay, a cascade that calls the 12B model only on the
\eFourCascGemmaTwelveMarTwentyCalls\% of questions where the vote's certificate margin is smallest passes an
unadjusted equivalence test (two points) against it. Voting succeeds where moment-based guarantees do not establish its
advantage; observed error dispersion supports a plausible mechanism, without identifying the entire gap to a binary
model. We
release an open application that turns any PC into a node of such a swarm, behind NAT, with an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Running large language models without a data centre is attractive for cost, privacy and resilience. The
dominant decentralised approach, pipeline-parallel serving of one large model over volunteers
\cite{borzunov2023petals,ryabinin2023swarm}, is bounded by latency: every generated token crosses the
network, so wide-area round-trip times of 50--150\,ms limit decoding to a few tokens per second.

We take the opposite stance. Each participant runs a complete small model (a few billion parameters;
\eSmallSizes{}B in our experiments, total counts) that fits on a consumer GPU, and participants deliberately run \emph{different} model families (Qwen, Gemma,
Granite, SmolLM, Mistral, Phi, OLMo). A request is broadcast once; peers answer independently; the
requester fuses the answers. Reservation precedes parallel inference in the encrypted application;
network communication stays outside the token decoding loop.

This design raises three questions that we answer in this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Quality.} Can a swarm of small heterogeneous models rival a single model several times
larger? (\S\ref{sec:e4})
\item \textbf{Protocol.} Is one parallel inference phase enough, or does fine-grained collaboration (writing the
answer together, block by block) pay for its extra round trips? (\S\ref{sec:e2})
\item \textbf{Guarantees.} What can be proved about early decisions, vote weights, appeals and incentives
in such a swarm? (\S\ref{sec:theory})
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A parallel inference protocol for heterogeneous swarms, after reservation, with an
\emph{exact and optimal stop certificate} under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}, a conservative
operational certificate and latency formulas. (ii) $K$-class Nitzan--Paroush weights for open answers,
with an empirical regularised adaptation based on wrong-answer collisions; the idealised rule illustrates
how peers below the binary threshold can remain useful (\S\ref{sec:e3}). (iii) A negative result for moment-based
ensemble theory: accuracies and error correlations do not determine the vote's accuracy, and error dispersion provides a
plausible mechanism for the observed gain (Theorem~\ref{thm:nonid}, \S\ref{sec:why}). (iv) Incentive conditions for
paid peers, an impossibility result for duplicate-and-compare audits (Theorem~\ref{thm:audit}), and the
effective audit rate of canary jobs, which vanishes for a peer that recognises them
(Corollary~\ref{cor:canary}). (v) A controlled comparison of seven small families with four single models of
\eFourRefSizes{}B on four benchmarks, with paired unconditional tests using approximate numerical maximisation
(\S\ref{sec:e4}): exploratory, unadjusted tests on pooled questions indicate that the swarm is non-inferior to the 9B
and 27B models within two points,
and it beats the 27B on MMLU-Pro, but it is inferior to Gemma-4-12B, which no selector among its members' answers can
reach on MATH-500; an
exploratory cascade that calls the 12B model on a small fraction of questions passes an unadjusted
equivalence test against it. (vi) An open application implementing the protocol (\S\ref{sec:system}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Decentralised inference.} Petals \cite{borzunov2023petals} and SWARM parallelism
\cite{ryabinin2023swarm} split one model across volunteers; we instead replicate whole small models and
use one parallel inference phase after reservation.
\paragraph{Ensembles of LLMs.} Self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency} votes over samples of one
model; ``more agents'' \cite{li2024moreagents} scales sampling and voting; LLM-Blender
\cite{jiang2023llmblender} and Mixture-of-Agents \cite{wang2024moa} fuse heterogeneous models with extra
learned or LLM-based aggregation stages. Our setting adds the network and its round trips as a first-class
cost, and asks what can be decided \emph{early} and \emph{exactly}.
\paragraph{Voting theory.} Optimal weights for independent voters are due to Nitzan and Paroush
\cite{nitzan1982optimal}; correlated voters are studied by Ladha \cite{ladha1992condorcet}. We give
$K$-class weights for open answers and show why second-order moments cannot settle the question.
\paragraph{Speculative decoding} \cite{leviathan2023speculative} reduces the number of sequential large-model
calls; our appeal and certificate reduce waiting in a broadcast protocol. \emph{Cascades} such as FrugalGPT
\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} query a larger model only when a cheaper one is unsure; we replay this with a swarm
as the cheap stage and the stop certificate as the signal (\S\ref{sec:e4}).

\section{Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
A requester holds a prompt $x$. It selects $n$ peers of distinct families, sends $x$ to all of them in one
parallel inference phase after reservation and key discovery (\S\ref{sec:system}), and receives answers $A_i$ (with a
confidence, the mean token log-probability) as they arrive.
Answers are normalised (final number, \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} content with the standard MATH
normalisation \cite{hendrycks2021math}, or option letter). The decision is a weighted plurality
\begin{equation}
\hat Y=\arg\max_c \sum_i w_i \mathbf 1\{A_i=c\},\label{eq:vote}
\end{equation}
with operational tie rules specified below. The weights $w_i$ are fitted once on a development split.
E2 and E3 choose the tied answer supported by the peer with the highest mean token log-probability,
then the first peer in their stored order. E4 first uses the highest supporting peer weight, then its
log-probability, then family order (scores within numerical tolerance are treated as tied). The application
uses the highest supporting log-probability, then lexical answer order. These answer-dependent rules
are distinct from the fixed total order in the following theorem.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{An exact and optimal stop certificate}
\begin{theorem}[Stop certificate]\label{thm:cert}
Let the weights $w_i\ge0$ and a total order $\prec$ for ties be fixed in advance, and let each peer
contribute a vector $v_i$ (a unit vector for a vote, a distribution for a linear pool). After receiving the
peers in $S$, let $L_c=\sum_{i\in S}w_iv_i(c)$, $R=\sum_{i\notin S}w_i$ and $a$ the first maximiser of
$L$. The full decision is necessarily $a$ if and only if, for every $b\neq a$, $L_a-L_b\ge R$ when $a\prec
b$ and $L_a-L_b>R$ when $b\prec a$. No exact procedure using the same received vectors and weights can stop earlier
while remaining correct for every admissible completion by the missing peers.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For any $b$, $\sum_i w_i[v_i(a)-v_i(b)]\ge L_a-L_b-R$, so the conditions make $a$ win against every $b$.
Conversely, if the condition fails for some $b$, the completion in which every missing peer answers $b$
makes $b$ win, while the completion in which they all answer $a$ makes $a$ win; two consistent completions
disagree, so no exact rule can decide.
\end{proof}
E3, E4 and the application instead certify only a strict lead over the runner-up plus all remaining
weight and a numerical safety margin, including unseen answers. This is sufficient and conservative;
Theorem~\ref{thm:cert} does not establish earliest possible stopping for their operational tie rules.

Here $T_i$ is the elapsed time from request initiation until peer $i$'s answer arrives, including its
reservation, key acquisition, relayed inference and generation when applicable. Replacement paths and
shared tracker load can couple these times; the following formulas assume a fixed set of peers.
With latencies $T_i$ independent of the answers and \emph{mutually} independent, with distribution
functions $F_i$, the expected decision time is
$\mathbb E[\tau]=\int_0^\infty\sum_{S}h(S)\prod_{i\in S}F_i(t)\prod_{i\notin S}(1-F_i(t))\,dt\le\mathbb
E[\max_iT_i]$, where $h(S)$ is the probability that $S$ does not yet certify (for dependent latencies the
product is replaced by the joint probability that exactly the peers of $S$ have arrived by $t$); for i.i.d.\ exponential
latencies $\mathbb E[\tau]=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k<n}\Pr(J>k)/(n-k)$ against $H_n/\lambda$ for waiting for all,
$J$ being the arrival rank at which the certificate holds. These formulas were checked by simulation
(appendix).

\begin{samepage}
\subsection{Vote weights for open answers}
\begin{theorem}[$K$-class weights {\cite{nitzan1982optimal}}]\label{thm:np}
If, given $Y$, the answers are independent, correct with probability $p_i$ and otherwise uniform over
$K-1$ wrong answers, the error-minimising rule is \eqref{eq:vote} with
$w_i=\log\frac{(K-1)p_i}{1-p_i}$ (plus $\log$ priors).
\end{theorem}
\end{samepage}
For open answers $K$ is not given. Under the model, two wrong peers coincide with probability
$c=1/(K-1)$, so we estimate $c$ on the development split and use $w_i=\operatorname{logit}(p_i)-\log c$.
On GSM8K, $c=\eThreeCollision$: agreement between two peers is strong evidence even when each is weak.
The binary weights $\operatorname{logit}(p_i)$ become negative below $1/2$; truncating them at zero in
practice silences these peers. Our empirical adaptation clips estimated probabilities and truncates
$\operatorname{logit}(p_i)-\log c$ at zero, using accuracy conditional on an extractable answer.
Neither this regularisation nor a global collision estimate establishes conditional independence or
uniform wrong classes. Bayes optimality holds under the theorem's model, not universally for this
adaptation. E3's final conditional accuracies do not demonstrate a gain from peers below $1/2$.
The shift $-\log c$ is common to all peers but still changes decisions, because an answer's score adds
one shift per supporter: with accuracies $(0.9,0.6,0.6,0.6)$, answers $(A,B,B,B)$ and $c=0.1$, binary
weights choose $A$ and $K$-class weights choose $B$.

\subsection{Why moments cannot explain the vote}\label{sec:why}
\begin{theorem}[Cantelli bound]\label{thm:cantelli}
Let $X_i=\mathbf 1\{A_i=Y\}$ with $\mathbb E X_i=p_i$, weights $a_i\ge0$, $W=\sum a_i$,
$d=\sum a_ip_i-W/2>0$ and $V=\operatorname{Var}(\sum a_iX_i)$. Then the vote is correct with probability at
least $d^2/(V+d^2)$, for any number of classes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Non-identification]\label{thm:nonid}
Even with two classes, the accuracies and all pairwise error correlations determine neither the accuracy
of the majority vote nor whether it beats the best voter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Two exchangeable laws of three voters with $p_i=0.6$ and zero correlations give majority accuracies
$0.84$ and $0.52$ (appendix).
\end{proof}
On our GSM8K data a binary model with the measured accuracies and independent errors predicts
\eTwoBinaryPred\% for the vote, against \eTwoVote\% observed, and the Cantelli bound is vacuous. Error dispersion is a
plausible contributor to the gap: wrong numeric answers spread over many values.
The vacuous bound does not predict failure, and dispersion alone does not identify the gap: dependencies,
abstentions and tie rules also differ from this binary model.
When two peers are right and two wrong, a binary model can only flip a coin, yet the vote was right in
\eTwoSplitTwoWon{} of \eTwoSplitTwoN{} such questions; a lone correct peer still won
\eTwoSplitOneWon{} of \eTwoSplitOneN{} times.

\subsection{Minority appeal}
\begin{theorem}[Appeal identity]\label{thm:appeal}
Let a second round on an event $E$ replace the fused answer $a$ by the runner-up $b$ with decision $D$.
With $B=\Pr(E,Y=b)$, $C=\Pr(E,Y=a)$, $r=\Pr(D=1\mid E,Y=b)$ and $h=\Pr(D=1\mid E,Y=a)$, the accuracy
gain is exactly $Br-Ch$, without any independence assumption; the optimal rule thresholds the likelihood
ratio of the appeal evidence at $C/B$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Incentives and audits}
\begin{theorem}[Honest serving]\label{thm:incentive}
If a job is audited with probability $q$ whatever the peer serves on it, an audit sanctions an honest
peer with probability $\alpha$ and a deviation $d$ with probability $\beta_d$, a sanction costs $P+R$
(penalty plus reputation), and payment does not depend on the strategy, honest serving is a best response
iff $q(\beta_d-\alpha)(P+R)\ge g_H-g_d$ for every deviation, where $g_H-g_d$ is the cost saved by deviating.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Duplicate-and-compare fails]\label{thm:audit}
Let honest answers be samples of a distribution $r$, and let an audit compare a served answer with an
independent honest sample and sanction on disagreement. A peer that always returns a modal answer is
sanctioned with probability $\beta=1-\max_yr(y)\le\alpha=1-\sum_yr(y)^2$, with equality iff $r$ is uniform on
its support. If this deviation is paid like honest serving and costs no more ($g_d\le g_H$), no audit rate
or penalty makes honesty strictly preferable: the deviation is at least as good, and strictly better as soon
as $g_d<g_H$, or $q(P+R)>0$ and $r$ is not uniform on its support.
\end{theorem}
If finding the modal answer costs more than one sample, Theorem~\ref{thm:incentive} can still favour
honesty (appendix). Audits must therefore use reproducible greedy decoding and verifiable answers, with a
measured baseline $\alpha$.

When requests are encrypted end to end, the tracker can no longer copy a real job to a second peer; it
audits with \emph{canary} jobs of its own instead, and the peer may try to tell them apart.
\begin{corollary}[Canary audits]\label{cor:canary}
Let a fraction $\pi$ of the jobs a peer receives be canaries, the only audited jobs. The peer flags each
job as a canary with probability $t$ if it is one and $u$ if it is not, serves the flagged jobs honestly,
and chooses its strategy on the set $U$ of unflagged jobs, assumed to have probability
$\pi(1-t)+(1-\pi)(1-u)>0$. Assume that the expected payment of a job in $U$ does not depend on the
strategy, and let $\alpha_U$ and $\beta_{d,U}$ be the probabilities that a canary in $U$ is sanctioned
when answered honestly or with deviation $d$, and $g_{H,U}$, $g_{d,U}$ the costs on $U$. Then honesty is a
best response on $U$ iff $q_{\mathrm{eff}}(\beta_{d,U}-\alpha_U)(P+R)\ge g_{H,U}-g_{d,U}$, where
\[
q_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Pr(\text{canary}\mid U)=\frac{\pi(1-t)}{\pi(1-t)+(1-\pi)(1-u)}.
\]
If canaries are indistinguishable from real jobs in the peer's view, every test has $t=u$ and
$q_{\mathrm{eff}}=\pi$. If the peer recognises every canary ($t=1$, $u<1$), $q_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$ and, whatever
$P$ and $R$, honesty on real jobs is a best response only if deviating saves nothing ($g_{d,U}\ge g_{H,U}$).
\end{corollary}
Detection therefore rests on indistinguishability in the peer's \emph{whole} view, contents included, not
only in the format of the messages (\S\ref{sec:system}).

\section{Experiments}
All models are open-weight with permissive licences (Apache-2.0 or MIT), served in 8-bit GGUF by
llama.cpp, fully offloaded to the GPU. Questions are split once into development and test halves; temperatures and vote
weights are fitted on dev. E1 and E2 nevertheless select the best observed member on test. Their bootstrap intervals
against
that member are descriptive, conditional on the selected comparator, and do not account for selection;
they are not intervals against a prespecified model. E4 ranks members on dev.
Intervals are paired bootstrap 95\% intervals over questions, except in E4, which uses score intervals
and unconditional tests with approximate numerical maximisation (\S\ref{sec:e4}). E3 reanalyses E2,
E5 reuses E1, cascades reuse E4, and E2/E4 share GSM8K questions from common partitions and sample prefixes.
Reuse across experiments limits interpretation as independent replications. Fitting and test partitions
remain separate within each experiment.
Model sizes are total parameter counts, embeddings included; Gemma~4 E2B and E4B are named after their
effective sizes (\eGemmaTwoEffective{}B and \eGemmaFourEffective{}B) but hold \eGemmaTwoTotal{}B and
\eGemmaFourTotal{}B parameters in total.

\subsection{E1: fusing four families on multiple choice}
Four \eOneSwarmSize{}B models (Qwen3, Granite~3.3, SmolLM3, Gemma~4~E2B) answer ARC-Challenge and MMLU-Pro with
exact letter probabilities over five balanced option orders; a calibrated linear pool (one temperature per
model, fitted on dev) gives \eOneArcFusion\% on ARC against \eOneArcBest\% for the best member observed on test
(\eOneArcGain{} points, \eOneArcGainCI) and \eOneArcRef\% for Qwen3-4B
(\eOneArcRefGain{} points, \eOneArcRefGainCI). On MMLU-Pro it gives \eOneMmluFusion\% vs
\eOneMmluBest\% for the best member observed on test (\eOneMmluGain{} points, \eOneMmluGainCI), but
falls below Qwen3-4B at \eOneMmluRef\% (\eOneMmluRefGain{} points, \eOneMmluRefGainCI). With a 768-token thinking budget
the fusion reaches \eOneThinkArcFusion\% on ARC (best member observed on test \eOneThinkArcBest\%, Qwen3-4B
\eOneThinkArcRef\%).

\subsection{E3: weights and early stopping}\label{sec:e3}
Replaying E2 with weights fitted on dev, $K$-class weights reach \eThreeKclassAcc\% (\eThreeKclassGain{}
points over the best peer selected on dev, \eThreeKclassGainCI), against \eThreeVoteAcc\% for an unweighted vote. Each
peer's $p_i$ is its accuracy among the questions it answered, and $c$ is measured on pairs of wrong answers
actually given: a peer without an extractable answer casts no vote, which under the model of
Theorem~\ref{thm:np} (abstention independent of the true answer) carries no evidence. On this data, binary
weights happen to give the same accuracy (\eThreeBinaryAcc\%); this is an empirical observation, not a
general equivalence. With the stop certificate, the requester waits for \eThreeKclassWaited{} of 4 peers on
average with these weights and \eThreeVoteWaited{} with an unweighted vote, and the decisions are identical
to waiting for all in \eThreeKclassSame{} questions.

\subsection{E4: seven families against larger models}\label{sec:e4}
\paragraph{Protocol.} The swarm has \eFourFamilies{} members, one small model per family: Qwen3.5-4B,
Gemma-4-E4B, Granite-4.2-3B, SmolLM3-3B, Ministral-3-3B, Phi-4-mini and OLMo-2-1B
(\eFourSwarmSize{}B total parameters each, \eFourSwarmParams{}B together). The references are four single
models of \eFourRefSizes{}B: Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma-4-12B, Ministral-3-14B and Qwen3.8-27B. Every model answers
every question alone, once, with greedy decoding and thinking disabled, from the same prompt per benchmark
(at most \eFourGsmMaxTokens{} generated tokens, \eFourMathMaxTokens{} on MATH-500), in \eFourQuant{} GGUF
served by llama-server (llama.cpp build \eFourBuild) with the model's own chat template, the path used by
the application. The runs were batched on one data-centre GPU; the swarm's decisions are computed offline
from these independent answers, replaying one parallel inference phase after peer selection. The benchmarks are GSM8K
\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH-500 \cite{hendrycks2021math,lightman2023verify}, ARC-Challenge
\cite{clark2018arc} and MMLU-Pro \cite{wang2024mmlupro} (the last two answered with a short chain of thought and an option
letter). For each, a fixed random sample (all of MATH-500) is cut once into disjoint development and test
halves of \eFourGsmN{} questions (\eFourMathN{} for MATH-500). Everything is fitted on dev, per benchmark: the $K$-class
regularised weights adapted from Theorem~\ref{thm:np} ($p_i$ is the accuracy among the answers given, $c$ the collision
rate of
wrong answers), and the ranking used by the ``best member'' and ``$k$ best'' baselines. All numbers are on
test. Grading follows Appendix~\ref{app:grader}; on MATH-500 a complete answer without any
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} falls back to the final expression stated in the text. As a robustness
check we re-grade MATH-500 with \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} only. Comparisons are paired over
questions: we report Tango's score 95\% interval for the difference of accuracies \cite{tango1998equivalence}
and verdicts from unconditional tests on the same statistic (approximately maximised over the nuisance
parameter):
superior or inferior when a one-sided test at 2.5\% rejects, equivalent when both one-sided tests against a
$\pm2$-point margin reject at 5\% (TOST), non-inferior when only the lower one does, and inconclusive
otherwise. The supremum over the nuisance parameter is computed numerically (a grid refined around its
maximum), without an error bound or certified global maximum. Strict level guarantees of the exact
unconditional test family require justified maximisation or a conservative bound; our computed values
are numerical approximations. The pooled tests treat the test questions of the four benchmarks as one sample; since the
benchmarks are fixed strata with different sizes and different systems' errors, the pooled model is
also approximate, and they weight benchmarks by size, unlike the means of Table~\ref{tab:e4}. There is no
correction for multiplicity (see Threats to validity).

\paragraph{Memory, compute and latency.} The comparison is between seven machines and one, and its terms
should be explicit. The seven members hold \eFourSwarmParams{}B parameters together (\eFourSwarmGB{}\,GB of
\eFourQuant{} weights), about as much as Qwen3.8-27B alone (\eFourQwenTwentySevenGB{}\,GB) and more than twice
Gemma-4-12B (\eFourGemmaTwelveGB{}\,GB). What the swarm changes is where the memory sits: no peer holds more
than \eFourLargestPeerGB{}\,GB of weights (plus its KV cache), within reach of common 12--16\,GB consumer
GPUs, whereas the \eFourQwenTwentySevenGB{}\,GB of weights of the 27B model exceed any consumer GPU below
32\,GB before any cache. With a decode-compute proxy (generated tokens times total parameters, prompt
processing ignored, averaged over the four benchmarks), the swarm costs \eFourComputeQwenTwentySeven{} times
Qwen3.8-27B, but \eFourComputeGemmaTwelve{} times Gemma-4-12B and \eFourComputeQwenNine{} times Qwen3.5-9B:
against the 9B model the swarm buys non-inferiority (not equivalence) with about three times the proxy
compute, and against the 12B model it stays below it with two and a half times, spread over seven owners'
machines. The proxy counts Gemma-4-E4B at its total size and is not a measurement of FLOPs, time or
energy. Per peer, a request costs one generation by a model of at
most \eFourLargestPeerParams{}B parameters. The application's latency follows overlapping per-peer paths from
reservation and key acquisition
through relayed inference and generation, until certification, with overheads and possible replacements
(\S\ref{sec:system}); in this replay the certificate spared \eFourAvoidedMin{} to
\eFourAvoidedMax{} answers out of 7 on average. We did not measure end-to-end latency on consumer hardware
in E4.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} Each model ran once, greedily: the results do not include sampling
variance or the run-to-run nondeterminism of batched inference, and greedy decoding without thinking
under-represents models that are trained to reason at length (both Qwen references, in particular). The
test halves have 250--300 questions, so per-benchmark intervals span 4--11 points and many verdicts are
inconclusive. We computed \eFourNComputed{} per-benchmark comparisons (weighted and plurality votes; the
\eFourNShown{} of the weighted vote are displayed) and 4 pooled ones, plus the cascade variants, without
multiplicity correction and without a prespecified primary hypothesis: the superiority over Qwen3.8-27B on
MMLU-Pro (one-sided $p=\eFourMmluQwenTwentySevenPSup$) would not survive a Bonferroni correction over the
\eFourNShown{} displayed comparisons (threshold \eFourBonferroniAlpha), and the per-benchmark verdicts are
best read as exploratory. The benchmarks are
public and may be in the training data of any model, small or large, to different extents. Answers are
extracted by rules (Appendix~\ref{app:grader}); the MATH-500 fallback changes one member's score a lot, and
the strict re-grading above is a sensitivity check of the vote and of the cascade under a second rule, not
a bound on grading error. The dev and test halves come from the same
distributions, so the fitted weights and cascade thresholds have not been tested under distribution shift,
and the same prompt was used for every model rather than each model's preferred format.

\subsection{E5: no demonstrated appeal gain with peer-sized judges}\label{sec:e5}
We tested the appeal of Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal} on the E1 multiple-choice data: when the runner-up of
the calibrated mixture is some expert's first choice, the most confident proposer is set aside and the
other three experts judge ``$a$ or $b$?'' blind, in both display orders; the answer is replaced when the
median order-averaged log-ratio exceeds a threshold chosen on dev. On MMLU-Pro test the appeal fires on
\eFiveMmluAppeals{} of 300 questions ($B=\eFiveMmluB\%$, $C=\eFiveMmluC\%$); judges recover
$r=\eFiveMmluR\%$ of the correct runners-up but break $h=\eFiveMmluH\%$ of the correct answers, for a gain
of \eFiveMmluGain{} points (\eFiveMmluGainCI), with no demonstrated gain in this protocol. On ARC the dev-chosen
threshold is so
conservative that only \eFiveArcFlips{} answer changes on test (\eFiveArcGain{} points). By
Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal}, an appeal pays only if $r>(C/B)\,h$; the estimated gain here is positive but uncertain.
A stronger judge or a verifiable check is a hypothesis for improving separation, not an established
necessity.

\subsection{E10: parallel sections trade judged quality for modelled latency}\label{sec:e10}
We tested a Skeleton-of-Thought scheme \cite{ning2023skeleton} on first-turn MT-Bench prompts
\cite{zheng2023judging}: Qwen3.5-4B writes a
skeleton, then its points are expanded round-robin across \eTenPeers{} heterogeneous peers and
concatenated without revision. Writing, roleplay, STEM and humanities provide \eTenDevN{} dev
prompts and \eTenTestN{} test prompts; the remaining \eTenOtherN{} prompts are reported separately.
The single model against which the parallel answer scores lowest on dev (soft probabilities break
ties), \eTenBestName{}, is frozen for test; it is also the skeleton model.
Qwen3.8-27B judges each comparison in both display orders. We average win/tie/loss scores across
orders per prompt, treating a win/loss reversal as a tie, and bootstrap prompts
(\eTenBoot{} resamples, \eTenCILevel\% intervals). Against the frozen solo, test gives
\eTenTestWin{} wins, \eTenTestTie{} ties and \eTenTestLoss{} losses, with mean score
\eTenTestScore{} (CI \eTenTestScoreCI; a score of one half denotes parity).
Order consistency is \eTenTestConsistency\%. This is one model's preference, not human evaluation
or verified correctness; residual judge and family biases remain, and these modest samples do not
establish equivalence. On the excluded categories, the corresponding score is
\eTenOtherScore{} (CI \eTenOtherScoreCI), with \eTenOtherFallback{} skeleton fallback.

Latency is \emph{modelled}, not measured network latency: solo time is RTT plus output tokens
at the model's decoding rate; parallel time adds the skeleton and the slowest peer's assigned
expansions, with two RTTs. Rates come from single-stream Q8 decoding on an RX~6650~XT where available;
\eTenDefaulted{} models (Qwen, Gemma, Ministral and Phi) instead use an assumed
\eTenDefaultSpeed{} tokens/s. At parameterized RTT \eTenRttMid{} ms, test mean times are
\eTenTestSeconds{} s parallel and \eTenTestSoloSeconds{} s solo, a latency ratio of
\eTenTestSpeedup{}; assuming \eTenUniformSpeed{} tokens/s for every peer gives
\eTenTestUniformSpeedup{}. The report also varies RTT from \eTenRttLow{} to \eTenRttHigh{} ms.
This model omits prompt processing, queueing and contention; batched generation times from the
campaign are not used as single-stream latencies.

Length budgets differ: solo answers allow \eTenBaselineBudget{} tokens, skeletons
\eTenOutlineBudget{}, and each expansion \eTenExpandBudget{}. Test answers average
\eTenTestTokens{} generation tokens parallel versus \eTenTestSoloTokens{} solo
(counts use each producer's tokenizer). On test, \eTenTestCut{} of \eTenTestExpansions{}
expansions and \eTenTestSoloCut{} solo answers reach their length cap; there are
\eTenTestFallback{} skeleton fallbacks and \eTenTestLongPoints{} point exceeding the requested
word limit. Thus the projected latency saving accompanies longer output and substantially worse
judged quality against the dev-selected solo under this protocol.

\subsection{E11: selecting code by execution}\label{sec:e11}
We generated \eElevenGenerations{} answers from \eElevenModels{} Q8 models on pinned HumanEval+ and MBPP+
exports \cite{liu2023evalplus,evalplushumaneval,evalplusmbpp}:
one greedy answer and \eElevenSamples{} samples at temperature \eElevenTemperature{}, with thinking

\section{System}\label{sec:system}
We release an implementation of the protocol as a desktop application (Python, llama.cpp).
\paragraph{Roles.} Every participant runs a \emph{node}: it serves one GGUF model chosen by the user with
llama-server, within user-set limits (concurrent jobs, pause/resume), and embeds a local
OpenAI-compatible \emph{gateway} (\texttt{/v1/chat/completions}) and a web interface, both bound to the
loopback interface. A \emph{tracker} acts as rendezvous, relay and credit ledger; it runs no model.
\paragraph{NAT traversal.} Nodes open a single outbound WebSocket to the tracker, so no inbound port is
needed on consumer connections; jobs and results are relayed over these sockets. Protocol 1.3 reserves
peers at the tracker; each assignment supplies certified keys and permits the requester to send encrypted
contents to that peer. Rejected or unavailable peers may require replacement exchanges. For each peer, reservation and
key acquisition precede its relayed
inference round trip and generation. These paths overlap across peers: an assigned peer can receive its
contents before all other reservations finish. End-to-end latency follows this critical path until the
certificate permits a decision, including processing overheads and any replacements; it is not a sum of
independent maxima for reservation and inference.
\paragraph{Identity and integrity.} Each node holds an ed25519 key; its identifier is a hash of the public
key. Login is a signed challenge; jobs are signed by the requester, results by the serving node, and
receipts by the requester, over canonical JSON with a message-type and protocol-version domain separator.
All fields are size-bounded and every job has a deadline.
\paragraph{End-to-end encryption (protocol 1.3).} Each serving node publishes a short-lived X25519 key
certified by its ed25519 identity. The requester derives per-job keys from a fresh ephemeral key (X25519,
HKDF-SHA256) and seals the request with ChaCha20-Poly1305 under a single-use pseudonym, padded to a
power-of-two size class; the result comes back sealed in authenticated chunks. The tracker routes and
settles credits from a signed plaintext header (target, token limit, deadline); it cannot read contents,
and altered or replayed messages are rejected. The serving peer reads the question in clear; the
requester's account is withheld from it, unless the content itself reveals it or the peer colludes with
the tracker. The tracker still sees metadata (payer, peer, sizes, timings).
\paragraph{Fusion.} The gateway selects $k$ peers of distinct families by reliability, broadcasts the
request, extracts answers when the task allows it (final number, boxed expression, option letter), and
applies regularised $K$-class weights and the conservative sufficient stop certificate described after
Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}; stragglers are
cancelled. Free-form answers fall back to a weighted medoid. The response carries the per-peer answers,
weights, decision rule and latency.
\paragraph{Credits and audits.} A served job earns completion tokens times a size factor; the requester pays
the sum over answering peers on a signed receipt. Encryption rules out the plaintext spot checks of our
first version, which duplicated real jobs to a second node. Audits now use \emph{canary} jobs: after a real
job relayed to a peer, with probability $q$ and after a random delay, the tracker sends that peer and
another node serving the same model file a question with a verifiable answer, sealed exactly like a real
job (same frame, header fields copied from the real job, same padded size class, fresh pseudonym, settled
as a receipt). It decrypts both answers and compares the extracted answers (a peer without a second node is checked
against the known answer); a truncated answer is believed only if it is long enough for its token limit,
and repeated truncations count against the peer. Following Theorem~\ref{thm:audit}, canaries are greedy with extractable answers, the honest
disagreement rate $\alpha$ is measured per model, and a node's reputation drops only when its disagreement
exceeds $\alpha$ by more than three standard deviations. By Corollary~\ref{cor:canary}, the deterrence is
$q_{\mathrm{eff}}$, not $q$, and it requires canaries that the peer cannot recognise. Our canaries use the same frame format, padding and settlement type as real jobs (checked by tests);
whether their metadata and timing (they follow a real job within seconds) can be told apart is not
measured, and they are \emph{not} indistinguishable by content: their questions come from a dozen public templates that a peer can recognise and answer correctly
while deviating elsewhere. Today they deter only non-adaptive cheaters, and only on the auditable class of
jobs; a peer that deviates on sampled or free-form jobs is never caught by a canary, and there only answer
fusion and quarantine protect the requester. Measuring adversarial canary detectors and generating
varied canaries are open work.
\paragraph{Limitations.} The tracker is a single point of failure and sees metadata; a tracker that runs
its own peers reads the questions it routes to them; token counts are bounded but not verified; Sybil
identities can collect the starter credit, and colluding peers can validate each other on canaries. The
roadmap replaces the central ledger by peer-verified receipts and the tracker by a federation, then a DHT
with direct NAT traversal.
\paragraph{Scalability (E7).} We measured historical protocols 1 and 1.1, not encrypted protocol 1.3.
The real tracker ran in its own process with up to 4{,}096 simulated nodes
(real WebSocket clients, signatures and gateways; answers drawn from historical per-family accuracies
with collision rate $c=0.09$, our first estimate of the GSM8K value, which counted abstentions as wrong
answers (\S\ref{sec:e3} gives the corrected \eThreeCollision); compute times drawn from the measured GSM8K timings) and a lognormal
wide-area delay injected on every relayed message. Both answer probabilities and compute times use older
synthetic parameters, not the final regraded results. RTT here is client--tracker: a relayed request and
response cross four delayed legs, totalling two such RTTs before compute. With the measured compute times, 256 nodes and
a
100\,ms RTT, the stop certificate lowers the median decision latency from \eSevenCertWaitAll{}\,s to
\eSevenCertCert{}\,s (\eSevenCertGain\%; \eSevenCertGainHetero\% with heterogeneous node speeds). With
compute times scaled down to load the tracker, a single tracker core serves, at the last unsaturated load plateau,
\eSevenRpsSixtyFour{}, \eSevenRpsTwoFiftySix{}, \eSevenRpsOneK{} and \eSevenRpsFourK{} requests/s at
64, 256, 1{,}024 and 4{,}096 nodes (RTT 100\,ms; p95 \eSevenPninetyfiveOneK\,s at 1{,}024 nodes).
Load plateaus last \eSevenStageSeconds{} seconds. At 64 nodes, the quoted throughput accompanies
\eSevenFailedSixtyFour{} failed requests, \eSevenAccuracySixtyFour\% synthetic accuracy (failures included),
and \eSevenAskedSixtyFour{} peers queried on average. These short windows establish tracker capacity
in this setup, not prolonged-load throughput, maintained inference quality or throughput of protocol 1.3.
A first version that let every gateway download the $O(N)$ directory capped at \eSevenRpsBeforeOneK{} requests/s at
1{,}024 nodes; selecting peers on the tracker in $O(k)$ from per-family indexes removed this bottleneck.
Hung nodes are detected by application-level pings and deadline misses and suspended with exponential
backoff. Beyond one core, trackers could be sharded by request hash (not evaluated), since selection needs no global state
beyond per-family indexes.

\section{Limitations}
Our swarm uses more total compute than one model of a single member's size: in E4, by a decode-compute
proxy, about as much as the 27B reference and two to three times as much as the 9B and 12B ones, for
exploratory unadjusted non-inferiority to the 9B and inferiority to the 12B. The claim
is about what consumer devices can achieve \emph{together} in one parallel inference phase after reservation, not about
compute efficiency.
The E4 comparisons rest on one greedy run per model, without thinking modes, on public benchmarks
(\S\ref{sec:e4}). Voting needs extractable answers; free-form text requires a similarity-based medoid whose
quality we have not evaluated. A non-significant difference is not equivalence; we use equivalence tests
with an explicit margin where we claim parity, and non-inferiority is weaker than equivalence. Canary
audits deter only peers that cannot recognise canaries, and today's canary contents are recognisable
(\S\ref{sec:system}). The credit ledger is centralised.

\section{Conclusion}
One parallel inference phase after reservation, regularised vote weights and a conservative sufficient
stop certificate give a swarm of small heterogeneous models exploratory, unadjusted non-inferiority
results within two points of single 9B and 27B models on pooled test questions of four verifiable
benchmarks (offline replay, greedy decoding, approximate numerical tests and fixed-stratum pooling), without putting the
network in the decoding loop and without any device holding more than
a small model. The swarm is not a free lunch: by our proxy it spends as much compute as the largest of
these models, it stays below the strongest 12B model we tested, and where all its members fail, as on part
of MATH-500, no selection among their answers helps. Calling a larger peer only when the certificate margin is small closed that gap in
our exploratory replay, which suggests networks where most requests are served by small nodes and a few by rare large
ones.

\section{Proofs and numerical checks}
All numerical checks below were run with exact enumeration or 400{,}000 Monte-Carlo draws; the scripts are
in the repository.

\paragraph{Latency of the certificate.}
The certificate is persistent: once the received answers certify $a$, any further answer keeps $a$
certified. Hence $\{\tau>t\}$ is the event that the set $S_t$ of peers arrived by $t$ does not
certify. If the latencies are independent of the answers, conditioning on $S_t=S$ leaves the law of the
answers unchanged, so $\Pr(\tau>t)=\sum_S h(S)\Pr(S_t=S)$; if moreover the latencies are mutually
independent, $\Pr(S_t=S)=\prod_{i\in S}F_i(t)\prod_{i\notin S}(1-F_i(t))$, which gives the formula of the
main text after integration. Mutual independence is needed for the product: with five unanimous
equal-weight peers and $T_1=\dots=T_5=Z$, $\Pr(Z=1)=1/4$, $\Pr(Z=3)=3/4$, all latencies are independent of
the answers but $\mathbb E[\tau]=2.5$, while the product formula gives $2.79296875$. Since
$\tau\le\max_iT_i$ almost surely, $\mathbb E[\tau]\le\mathbb E[\max_i T_i]$; when $\mathbb E[\max_iT_i]<\infty$,
the inequality is strict iff $\Pr(\tau<\max_iT_i)>0$. For i.i.d.\ continuous latencies the arrival order is a uniform
permutation independent of the order statistics, so $\mathbb E[\tau]=\sum_j\Pr(J=j)\,\mathbb E[T_{(j)}]$,
and exponential spacings have means $1/(\lambda(n-k))$. Check: five unanimous equal-weight peers certify at
$J=3$; theory gives $0.7833$ vs simulated $0.7835$ for $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ (waiting for all: $2.2833$ vs
$2.2824$), and $1.1543$ vs $1.1535$ for $\mathrm{Lognormal}(0,1)$.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:np}.}
By conditional independence, $\Pr(Y=c\mid A)\propto\eta_c\prod_ip_i^{\mathbf 1\{A_i=c\}}
\bigl((1-p_i)/(K-1)\bigr)^{\mathbf 1\{A_i\ne c\}}$; its logarithm equals the weighted score plus a term
independent of $c$, and the posterior mode minimises the zero-one risk. Exhaustive checks for three voters
($K=2$ and $K=3$) found no disagreement with the exact Bayes rule.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:cantelli}.}
If $Z=\sum_ia_iX_i>W/2$ the correct answer holds more weight than all wrong answers together and wins,
so the vote is correct with probability at least $\Pr(Z>W/2)$. Since $\mathbb E Z-W/2=d$ and
$\operatorname{Var}Z=V$, Cantelli's inequality gives $\Pr(Z\le W/2)\le V/(V+d^2)$. The vote beats the best
voter (accuracy $p_\star$) as soon as $d^2>p_\star V/(1-p_\star)$.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:nonid}.}
Take three exchangeable indicators and the two laws of $N=\sum X_i$ on $N=0,\dots,3$:
\[
\mathcal L_1=(0.16,0,0.72,0.12),\qquad
\mathcal L_2=(0,0.48,0.24,0.28),
\]
with successes placed uniformly. Both give $\mathbb E X_i=0.6$ and $\mathbb E X_iX_j=0.36$, i.e.\ zero correlations, but
$\Pr(N\ge2)=0.84$ under $\mathcal L_1$ and $0.52$ under $\mathcal L_2$. Taking $Y$ uniform on $\{0,1\}$ and
$A_i=Y$ if $X_i=1$, $A_i=1-Y$ otherwise realises both as symmetric binary channels.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal}.}
Per question the change in correctness is $D(\mathbf 1\{Y=b\}-\mathbf 1\{Y=a\})$; taking expectations gives
$Br-Ch$, questions where neither $a$ nor $b$ is correct contributing zero. For a randomised rule $d(z)$ the
gain is $\int d(z)\,[Bf_b(z)-Cf_a(z)]\,dz$, maximised pointwise by $d=\mathbf 1\{Bf_b>Cf_a\}$, i.e.\ a
likelihood-ratio threshold at $C/B$; the maximal gain is $\int(Bf_b-Cf_a)_+$. A Gaussian check with
$B=0.1$, $C=0.3$ gives the predicted optimum $t^*=\tfrac12\log3$ and gain $0.0492$ (simulated $0.0490$).

\paragraph{Grading.}\label{app:grader}
Generated answers are graded from the stored texts. For GSM8K the last answer statement wins, whatever
its form (including ``\textbf{The answer is 16.}'' and ``The answer is $N=195$''); MATH-500 answers are the
last \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, normalised with Hendrycks' \texttt{strip\_string} (units after a
number removed) and one exact canonical spelling per rational number, so that $0.25$, $1/4$ and
$\frac{2}{8}$ agree, for grading and votes alike; option letters come from an explicit answer statement or a line
holding only the letter. An earlier, stricter GSM8K grader missed the first two forms; all GSM8K numbers
in this paper use the final grader, re-applied to the stored texts without new inference.

\paragraph{Theorems~\ref{thm:incentive} and~\ref{thm:audit}.}
The expected utilities are $U_H=cL-g_H-q\alpha(P+R)$ and $U_d=cL-g_d-q\beta_d(P+R)$ (same payment $cL$),
so $U_H-U_d=q(\beta_d-\alpha)(P+R)-(g_H-g_d)$, which gives the condition; participation additionally
needs $U_H\ge0$. For the audit, two honest samples agree with probability $\sum_yr(y)^2$ while a modal
answer matches the reference with probability $\max_yr(y)$, and
$\sum_yr(y)^2\le\max_yr(y)\sum_yr(y)=\max_yr(y)$, with equality iff $r(y)=\max_yr$ wherever $r(y)>0$, i.e.\
$r$ is uniform on its support. Hence $\beta\le\alpha$, and
$U_d-U_H=(g_H-g_d)+q(\alpha-\beta)(P+R)$ is a sum of two non-negative terms when $g_d\le g_H$: whatever
$q$, $P$ and $R$, honesty is never strictly preferred. The deviation is strictly preferred when one term is
positive ($g_d<g_H$, or $q(P+R)>0$ and $r$ not uniform on its support); otherwise the peer is indifferent.
Without the cost condition the conclusion can fail: with $r=(0.6,0.4)$ ($\alpha=0.48$, $\beta=0.4$), $q=1$,
$P+R=10$, $g_H=1$ and $g_d=3$ (finding the mode costs more than one sample), $U_H-U_d=1.2>0$.
Example: with $q=5\%$, $\alpha=1\%$, $\beta=95\%$, $P=12{,}000$ credits and a 1{,}000-token job paying one
credit per token, honest serving earns 394 credits against 330 for cheating (simulated: 393.6 and 333.4).

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:canary}.}
On a job of $U$ the expected payment is the same under both strategies (assumption), and only canaries
can be sanctioned, so the expected utilities differ by
$q_{\mathrm{eff}}(\beta_{d,U}-\alpha_U)(P+R)-(g_{H,U}-g_{d,U})$ with $q_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Pr(\text{canary}\mid U)$,
as in the proof above; Bayes' rule gives its value, well defined since $\Pr(U)>0$. If the peer's view of a
canary has the same distribution as its view of a real job, any test (randomised or not) flags both with
the same probability, so $t=u$ and $q_{\mathrm{eff}}=\pi$. With $t=1$ and $u<1$, $q_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$ and the
condition reads $0\ge g_{H,U}-g_{d,U}$. The application pays by declared tokens and sanctions through a
reputation that accumulates over audits, so the corollary is a model of its incentives under these
assumptions, not a guarantee.
