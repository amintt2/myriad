\title{One Round Trip Is Enough: Decentralised Inference with Swarms of\\
Small Heterogeneous Language Models on Consumer Hardware}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Decentralised LLM inference usually splits one large model across volunteer machines, which puts the
wide-area network inside every decoding step and caps throughput at a few tokens per second. We study the
opposite design: every consumer device runs a \emph{whole} small open model of a \emph{different} family,
and a request costs a single network round trip, after which the answers are fused locally. We give exact
guarantees for this protocol: a stop certificate that returns the fused decision as soon as the missing
peers can no longer change it, which we prove exact and optimal; the Bayes-optimal $K$-class vote weights;
an identity for the gain of a minority appeal; and incentive conditions for a credit system, including an
impossibility result for naive duplicate-and-compare audits. Empirically, on GSM8K a swarm of four
\eTwoSwarmSize{}B models from four families reaches \eTwoVote\% with one round trip, against \eTwoBest\% for its
best member and \eTwoRef\% for a \eTwoRefSize{}B model, while collaborative token-level writing costs
\eTwoCroiseTrips{} round trips for no gain. We show why voting works where moment-based theory predicts it
should not: wrong answers of different families \emph{disperse}, so two agreeing peers almost always beat
two disagreeing ones. We
release an open application that turns any PC into a node of such a swarm, behind NAT, with an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Running large language models without a data centre is attractive for cost, privacy and resilience. The
dominant decentralised approach, pipeline-parallel serving of one large model over volunteers
\cite{borzunov2023petals,ryabinin2023swarm}, is bounded by latency: every generated token crosses the
network, so wide-area round-trip times of 50--150\,ms limit decoding to a few tokens per second.

We take the opposite stance. Each participant runs a complete small model (a few billion parameters;
\eSmallSizes{}B in our experiments, total counts) that fits on a consumer GPU, and participants deliberately run \emph{different} model families (Qwen, Gemma,
Granite, SmolLM, Mistral, Phi, OLMo). A request is broadcast once; peers answer independently; the
requester fuses the answers. The network appears once per request, not once per token.

This design raises three questions that we answer in this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Quality.} Can a swarm of small heterogeneous models rival a single model several times
larger? (\S\ref{sec:e4})
\item \textbf{Protocol.} Is a single round trip enough, or does fine-grained collaboration (writing the
answer together, block by block) pay for its extra round trips? (\S\ref{sec:e2})
\item \textbf{Guarantees.} What can be proved about early decisions, vote weights, appeals and incentives
in such a swarm? (\S\ref{sec:theory})
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A one-round-trip protocol for heterogeneous swarms with an \emph{exact and optimal stop certificate}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}) and latency formulas. (ii) $K$-class Nitzan--Paroush weights for open answers,
with $K$ estimated from the collision rate of wrong answers, which keep weak peers useful where the
textbook binary weights silence them (\S\ref{sec:e3}). (iii) A negative result for moment-based ensemble
theory: accuracies and error correlations do not determine the vote's accuracy, and the observed gain is
explained by error dispersion (Theorem~\ref{thm:nonid}, \S\ref{sec:why}). (iv) Incentive conditions for
paid peers and an impossibility result for duplicate-and-compare audits (Theorem~\ref{thm:audit}).
(v) An open application implementing the protocol (\S\ref{sec:system}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Decentralised inference.} Petals \cite{borzunov2023petals} and SWARM parallelism
\cite{ryabinin2023swarm} split one model across volunteers; we instead replicate whole small models and
communicate once per request.
\paragraph{Ensembles of LLMs.} Self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency} votes over samples of one
model; ``more agents'' \cite{li2024moreagents} scales sampling and voting; LLM-Blender
\cite{jiang2023llmblender} and Mixture-of-Agents \cite{wang2024moa} fuse heterogeneous models with extra
learned or LLM-based aggregation stages. Our setting adds the network and its round trips as a first-class
cost, and asks what can be decided \emph{early} and \emph{exactly}.
\paragraph{Voting theory.} Optimal weights for independent voters are due to Nitzan and Paroush
\cite{nitzan1982optimal}; correlated voters are studied by Ladha \cite{ladha1992condorcet}. We give
$K$-class weights for open answers and show why second-order moments cannot settle the question.
\paragraph{Speculative decoding} \cite{leviathan2023speculative} reduces the number of sequential large-model
calls; our appeal and certificate reduce waiting in a broadcast protocol.

\section{Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
A requester holds a prompt $x$. It selects $n$ peers of distinct families, sends $x$ to all of them in one
message, and receives answers $A_i$ (with a confidence, the mean token log-probability) as they arrive.
Answers are normalised (final number, \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} content with the standard MATH
normalisation \cite{hendrycks2021math}, or option letter). The decision is a weighted plurality
\begin{equation}
\hat Y=\arg\max_c \sum_i w_i \mathbf 1\{A_i=c\},\label{eq:vote}
\end{equation}
with ties broken by confidence. The weights $w_i$ are fitted once on a development split.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{An exact and optimal stop certificate}
\begin{theorem}[Stop certificate]\label{thm:cert}
Let the weights $w_i\ge0$ and a total order $\prec$ for ties be fixed in advance, and let each peer
contribute a vector $v_i$ (a unit vector for a vote, a distribution for a linear pool). After receiving the
peers in $S$, let $L_c=\sum_{i\in S}w_iv_i(c)$, $R=\sum_{i\notin S}w_i$ and $a$ the first maximiser of
$L$. The full decision is necessarily $a$ if and only if, for every $b\neq a$, $L_a-L_b\ge R$ when $a\prec
b$ and $L_a-L_b>R$ when $b\prec a$. No exact procedure can stop earlier.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For any $b$, $\sum_i w_i[v_i(a)-v_i(b)]\ge L_a-L_b-R$, so the conditions make $a$ win against every $b$.
Conversely, if the condition fails for some $b$, the completion in which every missing peer answers $b$
makes $b$ win, while the completion in which they all answer $a$ makes $a$ win; two consistent completions
disagree, so no exact rule can decide.
\end{proof}
With latencies $T_i$ independent of the answers and \emph{mutually} independent, with distribution
functions $F_i$, the expected decision time is
$\mathbb E[\tau]=\int_0^\infty\sum_{S}h(S)\prod_{i\in S}F_i(t)\prod_{i\notin S}(1-F_i(t))\,dt\le\mathbb
E[\max_iT_i]$, where $h(S)$ is the probability that $S$ does not yet certify (for dependent latencies the
product is replaced by the joint probability that exactly the peers of $S$ have arrived by $t$); for i.i.d.\ exponential
latencies $\mathbb E[\tau]=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k<n}\Pr(J>k)/(n-k)$ against $H_n/\lambda$ for waiting for all,
$J$ being the arrival rank at which the certificate holds. These formulas were checked by simulation
(appendix).

\subsection{Vote weights for open answers}
\begin{theorem}[$K$-class weights {\cite{nitzan1982optimal}}]\label{thm:np}
If, given $Y$, the answers are independent, correct with probability $p_i$ and otherwise uniform over
$K-1$ wrong answers, the error-minimising rule is \eqref{eq:vote} with
$w_i=\log\frac{(K-1)p_i}{1-p_i}$ (plus $\log$ priors).
\end{theorem}
For open answers $K$ is not given. Under the model, two wrong peers coincide with probability
$c=1/(K-1)$, so we estimate $c$ on the development split and use $w_i=\operatorname{logit}(p_i)-\log c$.
On GSM8K, $c=\eThreeCollision$: agreement between two peers is strong evidence even when each is weak.
The binary weights $\operatorname{logit}(p_i)$ ignore this and give zero weight to any peer below $1/2$.
The shift $-\log c$ is common to all peers but still changes decisions, because an answer's score adds
one shift per supporter: with accuracies $(0.9,0.6,0.6,0.6)$, answers $(A,B,B,B)$ and $c=0.1$, binary
weights choose $A$ and $K$-class weights choose $B$.

\subsection{Why moments cannot explain the vote}\label{sec:why}
\begin{theorem}[Cantelli bound]\label{thm:cantelli}
Let $X_i=\mathbf 1\{A_i=Y\}$ with $\mathbb E X_i=p_i$, weights $a_i\ge0$, $W=\sum a_i$,
$d=\sum a_ip_i-W/2>0$ and $V=\operatorname{Var}(\sum a_iX_i)$. Then the vote is correct with probability at
least $d^2/(V+d^2)$, for any number of classes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Non-identification]\label{thm:nonid}
Even with two classes, the accuracies and all pairwise error correlations determine neither the accuracy
of the majority vote nor whether it beats the best voter.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Two exchangeable laws of three voters with $p_i=0.6$ and zero correlations give majority accuracies
$0.84$ and $0.52$ (appendix).
\end{proof}
On our GSM8K data a binary model with the measured accuracies and independent errors predicts
\eTwoBinaryPred\% for the vote, against \eTwoVote\% observed, and the Cantelli bound is vacuous. The gap
comes from what the correct/incorrect indicators discard: wrong numeric answers spread over many values.
When two peers are right and two wrong, a binary model can only flip a coin, yet the vote was right in
\eTwoSplitTwoWon{} of \eTwoSplitTwoN{} such questions; a lone correct peer still won
\eTwoSplitOneWon{} of \eTwoSplitOneN{} times.

\subsection{Minority appeal}
\begin{theorem}[Appeal identity]\label{thm:appeal}
Let a second round on an event $E$ replace the fused answer $a$ by the runner-up $b$ with decision $D$.
With $B=\Pr(E,Y=b)$, $C=\Pr(E,Y=a)$, $r=\Pr(D=1\mid E,Y=b)$ and $h=\Pr(D=1\mid E,Y=a)$, the accuracy
gain is exactly $Br-Ch$, without any independence assumption; the optimal rule thresholds the likelihood
ratio of the appeal evidence at $C/B$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Incentives and audits}
\begin{theorem}[Honest serving]\label{thm:incentive}
If a fraction $q$ of jobs is audited, an audit sanctions an honest peer with probability $\alpha$ and a
deviation $d$ with probability $\beta_d$, and a sanction costs $P+R$ (penalty plus reputation), honest
serving is a best response iff $q(\beta_d-\alpha)(P+R)\ge g_H-g_d$ for every deviation, where $g_H-g_d$ is
the cost saved by deviating.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Duplicate-and-compare fails]\label{thm:audit}
Let honest answers be samples of a distribution $r$, and let an audit compare a served answer with an
independent honest sample and sanction on disagreement. A peer that always returns a modal answer is
sanctioned with probability $\beta=1-\max_yr(y)\le\alpha=1-\sum_yr(y)^2$, with equality iff $r$ is uniform on
its support. If this deviation is paid like honest serving and costs no more ($g_d\le g_H$), no audit rate
or penalty makes honesty strictly preferable: the deviation is at least as good, and strictly better as soon
as $g_d<g_H$, or $q(P+R)>0$ and $r$ is not uniform on its support.
\end{theorem}
If finding the modal answer costs more than one sample, Theorem~\ref{thm:incentive} can still favour
honesty (appendix). Audits must therefore use reproducible greedy decoding and verifiable answers, with a
measured baseline $\alpha$; this is what our application does.

\section{Experiments}
All models are open-weight with permissive licences (Apache-2.0 or MIT), served in 8-bit GGUF by
llama.cpp, fully offloaded to the GPU. Questions are split once into a development half (choices,
weights) and a test half (reported numbers). Intervals are paired bootstrap 95\% intervals over questions.
Model sizes are total parameter counts, embeddings included; Gemma~4 E2B and E4B are named after their
effective sizes (\eGemmaTwoEffective{}B and \eGemmaFourEffective{}B) but hold \eGemmaTwoTotal{}B and
\eGemmaFourTotal{}B parameters in total.

\subsection{E1: fusing four families on multiple choice}
Four \eOneSwarmSize{}B models (Qwen3, Granite~3.3, SmolLM3, Gemma~4~E2B) answer ARC-Challenge and MMLU-Pro with
exact letter probabilities over five balanced option orders; a calibrated linear pool (one temperature per
model, fitted on dev) gives \eOneArcFusion\% on ARC against \eOneArcBest\% for the best member
(\eOneArcGain{} points, \eOneArcGainCI) and \eOneArcRef\% for Qwen3-4B; on MMLU-Pro \eOneMmluFusion\% vs
\eOneMmluBest\% (\eOneMmluGain, \eOneMmluGainCI). With a 768-token thinking budget the fusion reaches
\eOneThinkArcFusion\% on ARC (best member \eOneThinkArcBest\%, Qwen3-4B \eOneThinkArcRef\%).

\subsection{E3: weights and early stopping}\label{sec:e3}
Replaying E2 with weights fitted on dev, $K$-class weights reach \eThreeKclassAcc\% (\eThreeKclassGain{}
points over the best peer, \eThreeKclassGainCI), against \eThreeVoteAcc\% for an unweighted vote. Each
peer's $p_i$ is its accuracy among the questions it answered, and $c$ is measured on pairs of wrong answers
actually given: a peer without an extractable answer casts no vote, which under the model of
Theorem~\ref{thm:np} (abstention independent of the true answer) carries no evidence. On this data, binary
weights happen to give the same accuracy (\eThreeBinaryAcc\%); this is an empirical observation, not a
general equivalence. With the stop certificate, the requester waits for \eThreeKclassWaited{} of 4 peers on
average with these weights and \eThreeVoteWaited{} with an unweighted vote, and the decisions are identical
to waiting for all in \eThreeKclassSame{} questions.

\subsection{E5: the minority appeal does not pay with peer-sized judges}\label{sec:e5}
We tested the appeal of Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal} on the E1 multiple-choice data: when the runner-up of
the calibrated mixture is some expert's first choice, the most confident proposer is set aside and the
other three experts judge ``$a$ or $b$?'' blind, in both display orders; the answer is replaced when the
median order-averaged log-ratio exceeds a threshold chosen on dev. On MMLU-Pro test the appeal fires on
\eFiveMmluAppeals{} of 300 questions ($B=\eFiveMmluB\%$, $C=\eFiveMmluC\%$); judges recover
$r=\eFiveMmluR\%$ of the correct runners-up but break $h=\eFiveMmluH\%$ of the correct answers, for a gain
of \eFiveMmluGain{} points (\eFiveMmluGainCI), not significant. On ARC the dev-chosen threshold is so
conservative that only \eFiveArcFlips{} answer changes on test (\eFiveArcGain{} points). By
Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal}, an appeal pays only if $r>(C/B)\,h$; judges of the same size as the experts do
not separate the two cases well enough. A stronger judge, or a verifiable check, would be needed.

\section{System}\label{sec:system}
We release an implementation of the protocol as a desktop application (Python, llama.cpp).
\paragraph{Roles.} Every participant runs a \emph{node}: it serves one GGUF model chosen by the user with
llama-server, within user-set limits (concurrent jobs, pause/resume), and embeds a local
OpenAI-compatible \emph{gateway} (\texttt{/v1/chat/completions}) and a web interface, both bound to the
loopback interface. A \emph{tracker} acts as rendezvous, relay and credit ledger; it runs no model.
\paragraph{NAT traversal.} Nodes open a single outbound WebSocket to the tracker, so no inbound port is
needed on consumer connections; jobs and results are relayed over these sockets. A request therefore costs
one relayed round trip to each selected peer, in parallel.
\paragraph{Identity and integrity.} Each node holds an ed25519 key; its identifier is a hash of the public
key. Login is a signed challenge; jobs are signed by the requester, results by the serving node, and
receipts by the requester, over canonical JSON with a message-type and protocol-version domain separator.
All fields are size-bounded and every job has a deadline.
\paragraph{Fusion.} The gateway selects $k$ peers of distinct families by reliability, broadcasts the
request, extracts answers when the task allows it (final number, boxed expression, option letter), and
applies the $K$-class weighted vote with the stop certificate of Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}; stragglers are
cancelled. Free-form answers fall back to a weighted medoid. The response carries the per-peer answers,
weights, decision rule and latency.
\paragraph{Credits and audits.} A served job earns completion tokens times a size factor; the requester pays
the sum over answering peers on a signed receipt. Following Theorem~\ref{thm:audit}, spot checks duplicate
a small fraction of \emph{greedy} jobs with extractable answers to another node serving the same weights and
compare extracted answers; the honest disagreement rate $\alpha$ is measured per model, and a node's
reputation drops only when its disagreement exceeds $\alpha$ by more than three standard deviations.
\paragraph{Limitations of v1.} The tracker is a single point of failure and sees request contents; token
counts are bounded but not verified; Sybil identities can collect the starter credit. The roadmap replaces
the central ledger by peer-verified receipts and the tracker by a federation, then a DHT with direct
NAT traversal.
\paragraph{Scalability (E7).} We ran the real tracker in its own process with up to 4{,}096 simulated nodes
(real WebSocket clients, signatures and gateways; answers drawn from the measured per-family accuracies
with collision rate $c=0.09$, our first estimate of the GSM8K value, which counted abstentions as wrong
answers (\S\ref{sec:e3} gives the corrected \eThreeCollision); compute times drawn from the measured GSM8K timings) and a lognormal
wide-area delay injected on every relayed message. With the measured compute times, 256 nodes and a
100\,ms RTT, the stop certificate lowers the median decision latency from \eSevenCertWaitAll{}\,s to
\eSevenCertCert{}\,s (\eSevenCertGain\%; \eSevenCertGainHetero\% with heterogeneous node speeds). With
compute times scaled down to load the tracker, a single tracker core sustains
\eSevenRpsSixtyFour{}, \eSevenRpsTwoFiftySix{}, \eSevenRpsOneK{} and \eSevenRpsFourK{} requests/s at
64, 256, 1{,}024 and 4{,}096 nodes (RTT 100\,ms; p95 \eSevenPninetyfiveOneK\,s at 1{,}024 nodes). A first
version that let every gateway download the $O(N)$ directory capped at \eSevenRpsBeforeOneK{} requests/s at
1{,}024 nodes; selecting peers on the tracker in $O(k)$ from per-family indexes removed this bottleneck.
Hung nodes are detected by application-level pings and deadline misses and suspended with exponential
backoff. Beyond one core, trackers could be sharded by request hash (not evaluated), since selection needs no global state
beyond per-family indexes.

\section{Limitations}
Our swarm uses more total compute than one model of a single member's size; the claim is about what
consumer devices can achieve \emph{together} at one round trip, not about compute efficiency. Voting needs
extractable answers; free-form text requires a similarity-based medoid whose quality we have not
evaluated. A non-significant difference is not equivalence; we use equivalence tests with an explicit
margin where we claim parity. The credit ledger is centralised in v1.

\section{Conclusion}
A single round trip, a vote with the right weights and an exact stop certificate let a swarm of small
heterogeneous models on consumer devices match larger models on verifiable tasks, without putting the
network in the decoding loop.

\section{Proofs and numerical checks}
All numerical checks below were run with exact enumeration or 400{,}000 Monte-Carlo draws; the scripts are
in the repository.

\paragraph{Latency of the certificate.}
The certificate is persistent: once the received answers certify $a$, any further answer keeps $a$
certified. Hence $\{\tau>t\}$ is the event that the set $S_t$ of peers arrived by $t$ does not
certify. If the latencies are independent of the answers, conditioning on $S_t=S$ leaves the law of the
answers unchanged, so $\Pr(\tau>t)=\sum_S h(S)\Pr(S_t=S)$; if moreover the latencies are mutually
independent, $\Pr(S_t=S)=\prod_{i\in S}F_i(t)\prod_{i\notin S}(1-F_i(t))$, which gives the formula of the
main text after integration. Mutual independence is needed for the product: with five unanimous
equal-weight peers and $T_1=\dots=T_5=Z$, $\Pr(Z=1)=1/4$, $\Pr(Z=3)=3/4$, all latencies are independent of
the answers but $\mathbb E[\tau]=2.5$, while the product formula gives $2.79296875$. Since
$\tau\le\max_iT_i$ almost surely, $\mathbb E[\tau]\le\mathbb E[\max_i T_i]$; when $\mathbb E[\max_iT_i]<\infty$,
the inequality is strict iff $\Pr(\tau<\max_iT_i)>0$. For i.i.d.\ continuous latencies the arrival order is a uniform
permutation independent of the order statistics, so $\mathbb E[\tau]=\sum_j\Pr(J=j)\,\mathbb E[T_{(j)}]$,
and exponential spacings have means $1/(\lambda(n-k))$. Check: five unanimous equal-weight peers certify at
$J=3$; theory gives $0.7833$ vs simulated $0.7835$ for $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ (waiting for all: $2.2833$ vs
$2.2824$), and $1.1543$ vs $1.1535$ for $\mathrm{Lognormal}(0,1)$.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:np}.}
By conditional independence, $\Pr(Y=c\mid A)\propto\eta_c\prod_ip_i^{\mathbf 1\{A_i=c\}}
\bigl((1-p_i)/(K-1)\bigr)^{\mathbf 1\{A_i\ne c\}}$; its logarithm equals the weighted score plus a term
independent of $c$, and the posterior mode minimises the zero-one risk. Exhaustive checks for three voters
($K=2$ and $K=3$) found no disagreement with the exact Bayes rule.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:cantelli}.}
If $Z=\sum_ia_iX_i>W/2$ the correct answer holds more weight than all wrong answers together and wins,
so the vote is correct with probability at least $\Pr(Z>W/2)$. Since $\mathbb E Z-W/2=d$ and
$\operatorname{Var}Z=V$, Cantelli's inequality gives $\Pr(Z\le W/2)\le V/(V+d^2)$. The vote beats the best
voter (accuracy $p_\star$) as soon as $d^2>p_\star V/(1-p_\star)$.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:nonid}.}
Take three exchangeable indicators and the two laws of $N=\sum X_i$:
$\mathcal L_1=(0.16,0,0.72,0.12)$ and $\mathcal L_2=(0,0.48,0.24,0.28)$ on $N=0,\dots,3$, with successes
placed uniformly. Both give $\mathbb E X_i=0.6$ and $\mathbb E X_iX_j=0.36$, i.e.\ zero correlations, but
$\Pr(N\ge2)=0.84$ under $\mathcal L_1$ and $0.52$ under $\mathcal L_2$. Taking $Y$ uniform on $\{0,1\}$ and
$A_i=Y$ if $X_i=1$, $A_i=1-Y$ otherwise realises both as symmetric binary channels.

\paragraph{Theorem~\ref{thm:appeal}.}
Per question the change in correctness is $D(\mathbf 1\{Y=b\}-\mathbf 1\{Y=a\})$; taking expectations gives
$Br-Ch$, questions where neither $a$ nor $b$ is correct contributing zero. For a randomised rule $d(z)$ the
gain is $\int d(z)\,[Bf_b(z)-Cf_a(z)]\,dz$, maximised pointwise by $d=\mathbf 1\{Bf_b>Cf_a\}$, i.e.\ a
likelihood-ratio threshold at $C/B$; the maximal gain is $\int(Bf_b-Cf_a)_+$. A Gaussian check with
$B=0.1$, $C=0.3$ gives the predicted optimum $t^*=\tfrac12\log3$ and gain $0.0492$ (simulated $0.0490$).

\paragraph{Grading.}\label{app:grader}
Generated answers are graded from the stored texts. For GSM8K the last answer statement wins, whatever
its form (including ``\textbf{The answer is 16.}'' and ``The answer is $N=195$''); MATH-500 answers are the
last \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, normalised with Hendrycks' \texttt{strip\_string} (units after a
number removed) and one exact canonical spelling per rational number, so that $0.25$, $1/4$ and
$\frac{2}{8}$ agree, for grading and votes alike; option letters come from an explicit answer statement or a line
holding only the letter. An earlier, stricter GSM8K grader missed the first two forms; all GSM8K numbers
in this paper use the final grader, re-applied to the stored texts without new inference.

\paragraph{Theorems~\ref{thm:incentive} and~\ref{thm:audit}.}
The expected utilities are $U_H=cL-g_H-q\alpha(P+R)$ and $U_d=cL-g_d-q\beta_d(P+R)$ (same payment $cL$),
so $U_H-U_d=q(\beta_d-\alpha)(P+R)-(g_H-g_d)$, which gives the condition; participation additionally
needs $U_H\ge0$. For the audit, two honest samples agree with probability $\sum_yr(y)^2$ while a modal
answer matches the reference with probability $\max_yr(y)$, and
$\sum_yr(y)^2\le\max_yr(y)\sum_yr(y)=\max_yr(y)$, with equality iff $r(y)=\max_yr$ wherever $r(y)>0$, i.e.\
$r$ is uniform on its support. Hence $\beta\le\alpha$, and
$U_d-U_H=(g_H-g_d)+q(\alpha-\beta)(P+R)$ is a sum of two non-negative terms when $g_d\le g_H$: whatever
$q$, $P$ and $R$, honesty is never strictly preferred. The deviation is strictly preferred when one term is
positive ($g_d<g_H$, or $q(P+R)>0$ and $r$ not uniform on its support); otherwise the peer is indifferent.
Without the cost condition the conclusion can fail: with $r=(0.6,0.4)$ ($\alpha=0.48$, $\beta=0.4$), $q=1$,
$P+R=10$, $g_H=1$ and $g_d=3$ (finding the mode costs more than one sample), $U_H-U_d=1.2>0$.
Example: with $q=5\%$, $\alpha=1\%$, $\beta=95\%$, $P=12{,}000$ credits and a 1{,}000-token job paying one
credit per token, honest serving earns 394 credits against 330 for cheating (simulated: 393.6 and 333.4).
